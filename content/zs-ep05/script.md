# 1. Two Hours

I gave myself two hours and one route.

I left at first light, when the shadows were long and the street was still the colour of dishwater. The plan was in my head and on my phone. Two streets east. A pharmacy, a small supermarket, a bakery with its shutter half down. In. Out. Back.

My stomach had stopped complaining a day ago. My legs had not. Every step was a negotiation, and I did not have the strength to argue. But I had a time limit, and a time limit is a kind of courage when you are too tired to find the other kind.

I did not take the backpack's whole weight. I took the bottle, the phone, the flashlight, and a strip of cloth for my hand. If I was not back in two hours, I would not be coming back.

# 2. The Route

I walked it the way a person walks across ice. Close to the walls, feet flat, weight low. I stopped at every corner and I counted ten before I looked.

The streets were not empty. There were zombies in doorways, standing with their heads down, like people who had been told to wait and had forgotten for what. There were more in the open space by the crossing. They did not look at me. They did not look at anything.

Each time I saw one, I made myself slow down. Not speed up. Slow down. A hurried step is a loud step.

I counted four groups on the way east. I went around all of them, and I added twenty minutes to the clock and did not let myself regret it.

# 3. The Shops

The row of shops was smaller than I had remembered. A pharmacy at the corner. A supermarket in the middle, one long front window, cracked from top to bottom. A bakery. A phone repair shop with a sign that said open, and a door that was not.

Five entrances, if you counted the loading bay. Too many. Rule two said never enter without a second way out, but it did not say how many were too many.

There were six of them. Four in the street in front of the supermarket, standing as still as lamp posts. Two near the pharmacy. They did not need to see me. If I made a sound, I would have six.

I looked at the clock. Fifty minutes gone.

# 4. Past Them

I did not go through the front. I went around the back of the row, along the narrow service lane where the bins stood, and I tried the loading bay door of the supermarket.

Locked. I tried the next. A side door, half open, held by a wedge of folded cardboard.

I took a small stone from the ground and threw it as far as I could, across the road, against a metal shutter. It rang like a bell.

The six turned. Slowly. Not together. First one, then another, and then all of them, drifting across the road toward the noise like iron filings toward a magnet. They did not run. They did not think. They went where the sound was, and they stayed there, pressing their hands against the shutter.

That was the thing I had understood on the first day, and I understood it in a new way now. They were not clever. I could use that.

I went through the side door, and I did not close it.

# 5. What I Found

Inside, the shelves were half empty and half untouched. People had taken what they could carry, and what they could carry was not what I needed. I walked slowly along each aisle, and I took only what I could name.

Four tins of food. A box of crackers. A plastic bottle of water, sealed, from the back of a lower shelf where no one had thought to look. One more.

In the pharmacy I found a small box with a red cross on the lid. Bandages, tape, a tube of ointment. I wrapped my hand properly for the first time in five days, and it was the most ordinary thing I had done since the alert.

On the counter, under a layer of dust, was a folded street map. The shop had given it away to customers for free. I put it in my jacket as if it were gold.

# 6. The Mark

On the way back, I took a different street. Rule two. Never use the same exit twice.

It was a quiet lane, behind a row of garages. At the end, a door that had once been painted blue. And on the door, in white chalk, a single mark. A short line, and across it a cross.

I stopped. I looked at it for a long time.

The chalk was fresh. Not days old. The dust around it had been disturbed, and below the mark, on the step, was a small metal can, rinsed clean, with a folded piece of paper held under it.

I did not touch it. I did not open it. I only stood there, in the shadow of the wall, with my heart making a sound I had not heard in five days.

Someone else had been here. Someone who knew how to leave a sign without leaving a sound.

# 7. Home Before Dark

I was back with eleven minutes to spare.

I shut the steel door and sat down on the floor with my back to it, the bottles against my knees, and the food spread out in front of me like a meal that did not belong to me. I ate slowly. A tin, a few crackers, half a bottle of water.

Two meals. A little water. A new bandage. A map.

And a mark on a door.

I did not sleep, at first. I looked at the map. I found the lane behind the garages, and I put my finger on it. Someone had stood exactly where I had stood. And they had left a sign that said, very quietly, that they were still alive.
