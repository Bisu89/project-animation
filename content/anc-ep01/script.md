# Thermopylae: Why 300 Men Held a Pass, and Who Sold It

On the morning of the third day, a Greek army of thousands picks up its shields and walks away.

It leaves a few hundred men behind.

Their king has ordered the rest to go. He stays, and so do the men who came with him.

Behind them, high on a mountain, Persian soldiers have found a path nobody guarded.

By midday the pass will be surrounded.

For two days, these Greeks have been the hardest thing in the world to move. By the end of the third, they are dead.

So how does a small force stop the largest army anyone has ever seen?

And how does it lose, after all that, to a goat track?

Most people will tell you it was courage, and then it was a traitor. Both are true. Neither is the real answer.

The real answer is geometry. And a plan with exactly one weak point.

Let's go back to the start.

## The invasion

It is the summer of 480 BC. Xerxes, king of Persia, is marching on Greece.

His father tried this ten years earlier and lost at Marathon.

Xerxes means to finish the job, and he has brought everything. Soldiers from every corner of an empire that stretches from Egypt to India.

A fleet to match. A bridge of boats across the Hellespont, because he did not want to wait for a ferry.

He has also spent years on preparation. Herodotus says food was stockpiled along the whole route, in depots built long before the army arrived. A canal was dug through the headland at Mount Athos so the fleet would not have to sail around a cape where a storm had wrecked his father's ships.

This is not a raid. It is a state moving house.

How many men? Herodotus says more than two and a half million.

Herodotus was a wonderful storyteller and a poor accountant. Modern historians say somewhere between one hundred thousand and a few hundred thousand.

Even the low end is the largest force the Greek world has ever faced.

And here is the part that matters. An army that size has a problem that has nothing to do with fighting. It has to eat and drink every single day. Herodotus claims some rivers were drunk dry as it passed.

That is an exaggeration, but it points at something true: a force this large cannot sit still for long, and it cannot spread out too thin. It has to keep moving along roads, and it has to keep winning quickly.

The Greeks cannot beat it on open ground. They know that.

So they look for a place where open ground does not exist.

## Two fronts, one plan

The Greek plan is not just one battle. It is two, at the same time.

On land, they will block the road south at a narrow pass called Thermopylae, the Hot Gates. At sea, a few miles away off the northern tip of the island of Euboea, a Greek fleet, with the Athenians providing most of the ships, will fight the Persian navy at a place called Artemisium.

The two positions protect each other. The Persian fleet cannot simply sail past and land troops behind the pass, because the Greek ships are in the way. The Persian army cannot simply march through, because the pass is blocked. If either front breaks, the other has to fall back.

Remember that. It will matter at the end.

## The Hot Gates

Here the mountains come down almost to the sea. In places, the road between cliff and water is barely wide enough for a single cart. Herodotus says exactly that.

One thing to know about the ground today: the sea has long since retreated, and the pass is now a wide, flat plain. If you visit, you will not see the place the Greeks saw.

The Greek coastline was far closer to the cliff, and the road squeezed through between them, past hot springs, which is how the place got its name.

Now look at what that does. Imagine the Greek line packed shoulder to shoulder in the narrowest part, and say, for the sake of the picture, that each man takes up about a meter of frontage.

In a gap of fifteen or twenty meters, that means perhaps fifteen or twenty men in the front rank. Behind them, more ranks, each one ready to step forward.

Now the Persians. A hundred thousand men can march toward that line. But only fifteen or twenty of them can actually fight at any given moment. The rest are standing behind them, watching, with nothing to do. They cannot go around. They cannot go over the mountain. They have to come through the door.

That is the whole trick. The pass does not make the Greeks stronger. It makes the Persians smaller. A hundred thousand men, squeezed through a space the width of a cart, are only a few dozen men at the point of the spear.

This is where Leonidas, king of Sparta, takes his stand. He brings three hundred Spartan soldiers, chosen for the job, and every one of them has a living son. Sparta does not expect them to come back. It makes sure their family names will survive them.

Around those three hundred gather thousands more. Men from Tegea and Corinth, Arcadia, Thespiae, Phocis and Locris. Herodotus counts about seven thousand in all. The Spartans are the most famous of them, and they are fewer than one in twenty of the men in the pass.

Why only three hundred Spartans? Because Sparta was in the middle of a religious festival, and Herodotus says the Spartans sent Leonidas and his bodyguard as an advance force, planning to send the rest of the army after the festival was over.

It was a dangerous way to fight a war. It tells you something about how seriously Sparta took the threat, and how little it expected the first battle to be decisive.

They repair an old wall across the narrowest point. Then they wait.

## The wait

Xerxes arrives, camps, and does nothing for four days.

Herodotus says he was waiting for the Greeks to leave. That may be true. A Persian king who has been told that the Greeks are weak, and who has brought an army that size, might well expect his enemy to run. But look at it from his side as well.

His fleet has just lost ships to a storm. His scouts have told him that the Greek army is small. A few days of waiting costs him little, and if the Greeks leave without a fight, it costs him nothing.

He sends a scout to look. The scout comes back confused. The Spartans in front of the wall are exercising. Others are combing their hair.

There is a Spartan exile in Xerxes' camp, a former king named Demaratus, and Xerxes asks him what this means. Demaratus answers plainly. Spartans comb their hair before a fight to the death. These men are getting ready to kill you.

Xerxes does not believe him. On the fifth day, he sends his army in.

Before the fighting starts, someone warns a Spartan named Dienekes that the Persian arrows are so many they will hide the sun. Dienekes replies, according to Herodotus, "Good. Then we will fight in the shade."

Hold on to that. We will come back to the shade. And we will come back to whether he really said it.

## Two days of failure

On the first day, Xerxes sends in the Medes, with orders to bring the Greeks to him alive.

They try. The Greeks have longer spears. They have bronze armor and big round shields locked edge to edge, so there is no gap to reach through.

A Greek hoplite in full kit carries something like twenty-five kilos of metal and wood, which is a terrible thing to wear in a race and a very good thing to stand behind in a corridor.

The Persians have short spears, wicker shields, and plenty of courage. Courage is not much use in a corridor.

Look at it as a matter of reach. A longer spear means a Greek can hit his enemy before the enemy can hit him. A big shield that overlaps with his neighbor's means there is no gap to hit back through.

A Persian soldier coming up to that line does not get to use his speed, or his skill with a bow, or his numbers. He gets to be stabbed.

Wave after wave goes in and comes back in pieces. Herodotus says Xerxes, watching from a throne set up to see the battle, leaped to his feet three times in fear for his army.

Then he sends in his best. The Immortals, ten thousand strong, the royal guard. They do no better.

Day two is the same. The Greeks fight in rotation, each contingent taking its turn at the front and then pulling back to rest. This is more important than it sounds. A man fighting in a shield wall tires fast.

Rotating the front rank means the Greek line is always fresh, and the Persians, who also rotate, are always walking into the same fresh line.

Some ancient writers add that the Spartans pretended to run, then turned on the men chasing them. A corridor punishes anyone who rushes into it.

Speaking of things that cost nothing, a like and a subscribe are free, and they keep this channel going. Ephialtes, as we are about to see, charged a fee.

By the end of the second day, Xerxes has the biggest army in history and no way forward.

And now ask the question a commander would ask. What does he do?

He cannot wait. The Greeks can hold this road for as long as they have food and men. The Persians cannot hold their position forever, because they also have a fleet that is fighting at Artemisium, and an army that eats and drinks in vast amounts.

Every day at the Hot Gates costs him something, and brings him nothing.

He cannot force the front. Two days of attacks have shown that.

So the only way to beat the Greeks is to take the pass away from them. And to do that, he needs to get men behind the Greek line.

Which means he needs a path.

And then a local man walks into the Persian camp.

## The betrayer

His name is Ephialtes. He comes from Trachis, the region right behind the pass. Herodotus does not describe him as a soldier or as a villain. He is a man who knows something, and who sees that somebody will pay for it.

What he knows is the mountain track. A path winds over Mount Kallidromon, behind the Greek position, and comes down at their rear. It is called the Anopaia. It is on no Persian map, and it does not need to be once somebody has been paid to walk it.

Herodotus says Ephialtes did it for money. He does not say how much, or whether it was ever paid.

Now here is the part most tellings leave out. The Greeks knew about the path. Local people from the region had told them about it when they first arrived. Leonidas had posted a thousand men from Phocis on the track to guard it. And Herodotus says the Phocians volunteered for the job.

So this was not carelessness. It was a decision, and a reasonable-sounding one. The path was long and difficult. It would take a large force many hours to climb it. A thousand local men who knew the mountain, in the dark, in an oak forest where every step makes noise, looked like enough.

It was not enough, and the reason is not complicated. A thousand men can stop a patrol. They cannot stop ten thousand of the best soldiers in the Persian army, led by a commander who has been given a guide.

That night, Hydarnes, commander of the Immortals, leads his men up the track. It runs through an oak forest, and the leaves crunch underfoot. The Phocians hear them long before they see anything.

They grab their weapons and run to a hilltop to make a stand. Herodotus says they thought the Persians had come for them. The Persians do not bother. They fire some arrows, and then they keep walking.

The Phocians have just saved the enemy a fight. And down in the pass, the Greeks are about to get the bad news.

## The weak point

Step back for a moment, because this is the real answer to the question we started with.

The Greek plan had one assumption. The plan said: the pass is the only way through. If that is true, a small force can hold it indefinitely.

But it was not true. There was another way, narrow and slow and long, but a way. The whole defense depended on it staying closed, and it was defended by a small force that had to guard a long, dark mountain path against an army with a guide.

Engineers have a name for this. A single point of failure. A system that works perfectly until one part fails, and then does not work at all.

Notice what this means for Ephialtes. We have been taught to see him as the man who destroyed Thermopylae. But a plan that can be destroyed by one man walking into a camp was a plan with a hole in it already.

Ephialtes did not create the weakness. He sold the Persians a map of it.

That is not an excuse for what he did. But it changes the lesson. The lesson is not "beware of traitors." Every army has men who can be bought. The lesson is: do not build a plan where one of them is enough.

## The choice

At dawn, a scout runs in with the news. Soon men are coming down from the mountain to confirm it.

The Greek commanders argue. Some want to stay. Some want to leave. In the end, Leonidas sends the allied contingents away. They still have an army worth saving and a war to fight.

Why does he stay? Herodotus gives two reasons. The first is an oracle that told Sparta either the city would be destroyed or a Spartan king would die. The second is simpler. Spartans do not retreat. They are trained from boyhood to hold their ground or die on it.

Historians are suspicious of the oracle, and with reason. Prophecies that fit the outcome tend to be written after the outcome. A cleaner military explanation exists. If the whole Greek army had tried to retreat in daylight with the Persian cavalry about to arrive, it could have been caught on open ground and destroyed.

A rearguard that holds the pass long enough lets the rest slip away. Leonidas may not have been choosing to die for honor. He may have been choosing to die so that the others could live.

Whatever the reason, the Spartans stay. So do seven hundred men from the small city of Thespiae, who refuse to leave a fight their allies are staying for. Four hundred Thebans stay too, though Herodotus says Leonidas kept them as hostages and that they surrendered at the end.

That is about fourteen hundred men against an army of a hundred thousand or more.

## The last stand

Leonidas does not wait to be surrounded. In the morning, he leads his men out of the narrow pass, onto the wider ground in front of it, and fights there.

Not to win. To kill as many as they can, and to buy time for the rest of the army to get away.

Think about what that means. Everything that made the pass work for the Greeks, the narrow front, is gone the moment they step out of it. They do it anyway, because the pass is no longer holding.

It has been turned. The only thing left to do is to hurt the enemy as badly as possible, for as long as possible.

They fight with spears until the spears break. Then swords. Leonidas falls, and the battle turns into a fight over his body. Herodotus says the Greeks drove the Persians back four times and finally dragged it away.

Then the Immortals arrive from behind. The Greeks fall back to a small hill and make their last stand there. Men with no swords left fight with their hands and teeth. Persian archers and javelin throwers finish the job from a distance.

Remember the shade? Dienekes was right. That day, the sky does go dark with arrows. Whether Dienekes ever said it, we do not know.

The line comes from Herodotus, who was writing a generation later, and it sounds a lot like the kind of thing people say about a battle once they know how it ended.

But archaeologists have found Persian arrowheads on the hill, so the arrows at least are real.

Afterward, Xerxes has Leonidas's head cut off and fixed on a stake. Herodotus notes this was a strange thing for a Persian king to do, because Persians honored brave enemies. He takes it as a sign of how badly Thermopylae shook Xerxes.

Herodotus also tells a story that, if true, says even more. Xerxes, he says, buried most of his own dead and left only a thousand bodies lying out, so that visitors would see how many Greeks had died and how few Persians.

Herodotus did not believe the trick fooled anyone. Neither should we. A man who hides his casualties is a man who knows what they look like.

## What did it actually buy?

Here is where most tellings stop, with a poem and a moment of silence. But there is a harder question. Did Thermopylae work?

Look at it as a military operation. The Greek plan was to hold the pass. They did not hold the pass. In that sense, it failed.

Look at what it cost the Persians. A few days. A good number of dead, though nobody knows how many. And one more thing: the Greek fleet at Artemisium, which had fought the Persian navy to a bruising draw over the same three days, now heard that the pass had fallen.

With the land route open, there was nothing left to protect, and the fleet withdrew south.

So the Persians went on, and they burned Athens.

If that were the end of the story, Thermopylae would be a brave failure. But it is not the end.

The Greek fleet withdrew to the narrow waters off the island of Salamis. And there, a few weeks later, the same thing happened that had happened at the Hot Gates. The Persian fleet, far larger, was lured into a narrow channel, where its numbers were no use, and the Greeks destroyed it.

Xerxes had watched the battle from a throne on the shore. He went home soon after, leaving part of his army behind. That army was beaten at Plataea the following year.

Do you see it? Thermopylae did not win the war. But it taught the Greeks the shape of the war they could win. Not a battle on open ground, but a battle where the ground did the fighting for them.

They tried it at the pass, and it nearly worked. They tried it again at Salamis, and it did.

There was a second gift, harder to measure. Before Thermopylae, many Greeks believed the Persians could not be stopped. After it, they had seen a few thousand men do it for two days. That is the kind of thing an army carries into its next fight.

So was it worth it? You can argue both ways. The men who died did not get to see Salamis. But the Greeks who did see it had been told, by those men, what to do.

## What came after

Before the end, the Spartans had sent three men away, two with an eye disease and one on a message. Herodotus says one of the sick men went back into the fight and died. The other two lived, and were treated as outcasts at home.

One of them, Aristodemus, is said to have redeemed himself a year later at Plataea, fighting with a kind of fury that his fellow Spartans did not recognize as courage.

Later, the Greeks put up a stone at the pass with a poem by Simonides:

*Go tell the Spartans, stranger passing by, that here, obedient to their laws, we lie.*

And Ephialtes? He fled when the news got out, with a price on his head. Herodotus says he was eventually killed, though over something else entirely. The Spartans paid the reward to the man who killed him anyway.

## The point

Thermopylae is not really a story about bravery. The Persians had plenty of that too.

It is a story about a place, and what a place can do to an army. A narrow road made three hundred as strong as three hundred thousand. Then a narrow path made them as weak as three hundred.

The Greeks never lost the pass in a fair fight. They lost it when somebody showed the enemy a way around.

If you take one thing from the Hot Gates, take this. Counting the men in a fight will often mislead you. Count how many of them can reach each other.

And count the paths nobody is watching.
