On the frozen sea ice, a hunter kneels and cuts square blocks from hard snow. The wind that numbs his fingers is the same material he is about to live inside.

Shelter from the cold, built out of the cold. This is how some Inuit hunters in the Canadian Arctic and northern Greenland turned snow into a winter hunting shelter, not a year-round home, and not a technique every Arctic people used.

Snow looks like the enemy of warmth, cold, wet, everywhere you don't want to be stuck in winter.

But snow isn't the real problem. Heat loss is. Warmth doesn't vanish on its own; it leaks toward anything colder. The real question isn't how to beat the cold. It's what stops the heat from leaving.

Snow is mostly trapped air. Pack a handful and most of what you're holding is empty space between ice crystals, space that doesn't let heat move through easily.

It's the same idea behind a down jacket, or a window with two panes and a gap of air between them.

Fresh snow conducts heat about as slowly as still air. Solid ice conducts heat around seventy times faster. That difference is the whole trick: a wall built from packed snow holds warmth in, while the same wall built from solid ice would let it pour straight through.

Builders cut blocks straight from hard snowpack and stack them in a spiral, each ring leaning slightly inward until the dome seals shut at the top. Inside, the air warms from body heat alone, melting a thin inner layer of the walls. That meltwater refreezes into a thin shell of ice, locking the blocks together and making the whole dome stronger.

Exactly how warm it gets inside depends on the snow, the wind, and the weather outside, there's no single fixed number.

Back on the sea ice, that hunter isn't just cutting blocks, he's building a wall of trapped air to keep his own warmth from escaping. Your coat does the same job tonight, and so does the glass in your window. Warmth doesn't need walls of stone. It just needs air that can't move.
