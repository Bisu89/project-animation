# Agincourt 1415: historical notes (scope: scene 1 prototype)

**Verification status:** written from general knowledge. No network access was available in this environment, so none of
this has been checked against the sources below. Treat it as a checklist to verify before publishing, not as citations.

## Established facts (widely agreed, verify against the sources)
- The battle was fought on 25 October 1415 (feast of Saints Crispin and Crispinian) near Azincourt in northern France,
  during the Hundred Years' War.
- The English army was led by Henry V on a march toward Calais after the siege of Harfleur; it was tired and had lost men to
  illness and attrition.
- The French army that opposed it was the larger one. The exact numbers on both sides are disputed.
- The field was a relatively narrow strip between woods, recently ploughed and soft/wet underfoot.
- English longbowmen were a major part of the army; stakes were used by the archers as protection (to verify the details).

## Disputed or uncertain (keep out of authoritative narration, or label as estimates)
- Troop numbers and the true ratio between the armies; casualty figures.
- Exact deployment details: depth of the French lines, positions of cavalry wings, where the archers stood.
- The precise start time and how long the armies faced each other before fighting began.
- The relative weight of mud, congestion, archery and command decisions in the outcome. Do not credit longbows or mud alone.

## Creative staging used in the prototype (not claims)
- Dawn light, haze and the low sun are atmosphere only.
- Armies drawn as dense blocks of identical stick soldiers, with banners and a few mounted knights on the French flank.
- Colours are symbolic: red cross on white for the English, blue with gold marks for the French. Soldiers are generic figures;
  the two foreground commanders are unnamed and unlabelled.
- Stakes are drawn in front of the English line; their number and placement are illustrative.

## Sources to consult
- Anne Curry, *Agincourt: A New History* (2005).
- Juliet Barker, *Agincourt: The King, the Campaign, the Battle* (2005).
- Contemporary chronicles, e.g. the *Gesta Henrici Quinti*, and French chroniclers such as Monstrelet and Wavrin.
- Matthew Bennett's and Clifford Rogers's articles on the battle and on longbow tactics.
