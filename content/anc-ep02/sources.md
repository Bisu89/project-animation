# Ep 2 Cannae: source notes

Draft script: `script.md` (about 3,050 words, about 20 min). Evidence categories, so the narration never states a tradition as fact.

## Well attested (Polybius Book 3; Livy Book 22; later Plutarch, Appian)
- Hannibal's invasion of Italy, Trebia (Dec 218) and Lake Trasimene (June 217). Polybius 3.64-74, 3.82-85; Livy 21.54-56, 22.4-7.
- Fabius Maximus as dictator, strategy of delay, nickname Cunctator. Livy 22.8-18; Plutarch, Fabius.
- Election of Varro and Paullus for 216; very large levy. Polybius 3.106-107; Livy 22.34-38, 22.36.
- Roman strength: Polybius 3.107 and 3.113 give about 80,000 foot and 6,000 horse (8 legions of 5,000 plus equal allies). Livy 22.36 gives different totals. Hannibal about 40,000 foot, 10,000 horse (Polybius 3.114).
- Hannibal seized the grain depot at Cannae and camped on the Aufidus. Polybius 3.107; Livy 22.43.
- Consuls commanded on alternate days; Varro held command on the day of battle; Paullus disagreed. Polybius 3.110; Livy 22.41-45.
- Wind (Volturnus) and dust. Polybius 3.113-114.
- Roman infantry deployed deeper and with narrower intervals than usual. Polybius 3.113.
- Hannibal's crescent, Libyans on the ends, Spanish and Gallic cavalry on the river side, Numidians opposite the allied horse. Polybius 3.113-114.
- Course of the battle: Hasdrubal's cavalry defeats the Roman citizen horse, rides behind the line and routs the allied horse, then hits the infantry rear; crescent retreats; Libyans wheel in; double envelopment. Polybius 3.115-116; Livy 22.46-48.
- Casualties: Polybius 3.117 about 70,000 Roman killed; Livy 22.49 about 48,200 killed; Hannibal's losses about 5,700 (Polybius) or 8,000 (Livy).
- Paullus killed, refusing a horse (Lentulus episode): Livy 22.49.
- Varro escaped to Venusia with a small band of horse: Polybius 3.116 (70), Livy 22.49 (50).
- Maharbal's remark: Livy 22.51.
- Senate refused to ransom the prisoners; 30-day limit on mourning; 8,000 slaves bought and armed; Varro thanked for not despairing of the Republic. Livy 22.57-61. Polybius 6.58 on the refusal.
- Survivors posted to Sicily and denied return: Livy 23.25, 25.5-7 (the "Cannae legions").
- Capua and several southern allies went over to Hannibal; Latin allies mostly held. Livy 23.1-10.
- Hannibal near Rome in 211: Livy 26.7-11. He left Italy in 203; Zama 202: Polybius 15.

## Disputed, later, or interpretation (script uses hedged wording)
- Livy's portrait of Varro as a reckless demagogue is probably hostile and exaggerated (Livy writing 200 years later; Polybius is more balanced). Script says so.
- Casualty and strength figures: modern estimates vary (Roman dead often given as 50,000-60,000+; some argue the Roman army was smaller). Script gives "between about 48,000 and 70,000" and says modern historians are cautious.
- Maharbal's line and Hannibal's reply: probably a later literary invention. Script says "historians doubt it ever happened".
- Hannibal's war aim (break the Roman alliance, not capture Rome): common modern view, stated as "most historians think".
- "Cannae legions" exile: Livy; precise terms debated.
- Schlieffen's study of Cannae and the 1914 plan "often described as an attempt to repeat Cannae": Schlieffen's own book "Cannae" (1913, collected writings). The claim that the 1914 plan copied Cannae is a popular simplification; keep "often described".
- Scipio at Zama using a cavalry return to the enemy rear: Polybius 15.14 describes it; the link to Hannibal's Cannae method is an interpretation. Scipio's presence at Cannae as a young tribune: Livy 22.53 (and others).
- Dust blowing into Roman faces and the exact river/wing arrangement: Polybius; modern reconstructions differ on the exact site of the battle.

## Our own analysis (not from the sources)
- "Hannibal used the Roman strengths as the weapon" and the three-part explanation of why it worked (cavalry, discipline, Roman choices).
- The geometry framing and the callback to Thermopylae ("count how many can reach each other"); the "80,000 men fighting with the hands of perhaps ten thousand" figure is an illustration, not a source figure.
- The strategic conclusion (tactical masterpiece, strategic failure; Rome could absorb the loss).
