25 October 1415, northern France.

A tired English army, far from home, faces a much larger French force.

Between them lies a narrow, muddy field.

Henry V chose this ground on purpose.

A gap between two woods was too narrow for the larger French army to use its full width.

Archers lined both flanks, stakes driven into the earth in front of them.

The English longbow could loose arrows faster than almost any weapon of its age.

Volley after volley arced into the French ranks.

The French men-at-arms advanced across a field churned to mud by rain and thousands of feet.

The narrow ground crowded them together, rank pressing into rank.

When the lines finally met, the press of bodies left little room to even raise a weapon.

Exhausted and too tightly packed to manoeuvre, the French formation began to break apart.

It became one of the most one-sided defeats in French history.

Historians still debate how much was the mud, how much the arrows, how much the ground itself.

What is clear is that Henry's choice of terrain shaped everything that followed.
