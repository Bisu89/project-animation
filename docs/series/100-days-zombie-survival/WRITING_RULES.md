# WRITING RULES — 100 Days: Zombie Survival (do NOT skip; set by the owner 2026-10-20)

Applies to every new or refactored episode (Episode 1 was approved before these rules; keep it as is unless asked). Script in natural English; notes and explanations to the owner in Vietnamese.
If asked for a Vietnamese version: translate close to the meaning, in natural speech, no literary flourish.

## Style goal
First-person ordinary person trying to survive, telling what they did. Plain, clear, easy to follow on the first listen.
Prefer: concrete actions; clear needs (water, food, place to sleep, route, health); decisions with an understandable reason; consequences that follow from those decisions; short-to-medium sentences that sound natural aloud; mostly chronological order.
Create tension with realistic situations, not with fancy wording.

## Avoid (12 rules)
1. **No purple prose or vague philosophy.** Not "I was afraid of the arithmetic", "silence is information", "the city forgot how to wake". Say the concrete thing: "There isn't enough water for tomorrow." / "I stood still and listened for footsteps." / "No cars were moving and no shops were open."
2. **Never make the viewer guess the basics.** Always clear: where the character is, what they want, what blocks them, why they choose this way, what happened. The cause of the outbreak or unknowns may stay secret; never use murky lines to hide missing logic.
3. **No default flash-forward cold open.** Open with the concrete problem of the day. Time jumps only when asked or clearly useful and without giving away the climax.
4. **The narrator may not know what has no evidence.** No bus visible does not mean "the buses left". Arriving somewhere does not tell you an alarm has rung since yesterday. Watching one zombie for a few minutes does not prove the whole species has no memory. Separate: what I saw / what I guess / what I don't know.
5. **Zombies are not a plot convenience.** Keep hearing, sight, speed, reactions and behaviour consistent with what has been shown. If the character escapes thanks to another sound, show exactly what happened; do not claim it always works after one time.
6. **The character does not forget old lessons just to create danger.** Mistakes can come from hunger, tiredness, hurry, wanting rescue — always with a stated, concrete reason. If they knew to check a second exit and still went in, that is ignoring a known risk, not discovering the lesson for the first time.
7. **No stacking of random accidents.** Injuries, lost items, wrong turns, being spotted all need a cause, and each incident must change a later decision or the chance of surviving.
8. **No habitual last-second rescues.** Distracting noise, a door suddenly opening, someone arriving must not become the normal solution. Let the character observe and handle it. If it is luck, say it is luck.
9. **Not a survival handbook.** Weave experience into action and result. No long lectures. Do not present wound-care or dehydration advice as correct for every case without basis.
10. **Do not overuse fragments and line breaks.** Natural narration rhythm; very short sentences only for emphasis. The whole script must not sound like a movie trailer.
11. **No precise figures the narrator cannot know.** Exact times come from a clock/phone; distances from a road sign, map or an estimate that is said to be an estimate. Check travel time, stop time, water amounts and distance for plausibility.
12. **No cool-but-unclear endings.** End with the concrete state and what to do next. Example: "There isn't enough water for tomorrow. I have to find a source nearby before my ankle gets worse."

## Continuity checklist (before finishing an episode)
Start and end location; items carried / used / lost / found; water and food left; wound state and mobility; phone battery, signal, ways to communicate; what the character really knows; lessons from earlier episodes; zombie behaviour already shown. If important information is missing, flag it for the owner; do not invent facts that change an earlier episode.

## Suggested structure
Start: current state and today's goal. Middle: attempt, obstacle, adjustment. Climax: one decision with a clear consequence. End: inventory of results, current state, next need. Not every episode must fail or end with an injury: allow progress, found items, or a lesson applied successfully.

## Output format when refactoring
1. Short Vietnamese review: logic and style problems. 2. Complete English script usable for voice. 3. Continuity points to confirm.
